# 9. The Hiring-Manager Round and Closing Well

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter, you should be able to:

- tell your career story in two minutes, with a clear direction;
- answer "why this company?" and "why are you leaving?" honestly and positively;
- present a past project in depth for a hiring manager;
- ask questions that are useful to you and informative to the interviewer; and
- recognise and recover from the red flags that end otherwise good rounds.

## Why this matters at SDE-2

The hiring-manager round is where the question "do I want this person on my team?" is answered most directly. It blends behavioral questions with a project deep dive and a conversation about motivation. Candidates prepare the stories and forget the rest: the opening "tell me about yourself", the "why here?", and the questions they ask at the end - all of which the manager is also scoring.

## First-principles model

The hiring manager is assessing three things beyond the behavioral signals:

1. **Trajectory:** are you growing, and in a direction this team needs?
2. **Motivation:** do you want this role specifically, for reasons that will survive the first hard month?
3. **Team fit in the working sense:** how you would collaborate with this team on this kind of work - not personality.

Everything in this chapter serves one of those three.

## Core terminology

- **Career narrative:** a short account of your path that explains your choices and points forward.
- **Project deep dive:** a detailed discussion of one past project, usually technical and behavioral at once.
- **Reverse interview:** the questions you ask the interviewer.
- **Red flag:** a behaviour that outweighs otherwise good evidence.

## Detailed mechanics

### "Tell me about yourself"

Two minutes, in three parts:

1. **Now:** your current role and scope in one or two sentences, with one concrete achievement.
2. **How you got here:** two or three choices, each with the reason - what you were looking for, and what you learned.
3. **Why this role:** where you are heading and how this role fits.

A backend engineer's version: "I'm a backend engineer at a payments company, where I own the order and checkout services - most recently I moved order data onto its own database without downtime. I started in a services consultancy, moved to product engineering to own systems long term, and have spent the last three years on distributed systems and reliability. I'm looking for a team where I can take on larger system ownership, which is why this platform role stood out."

It is not a resume walk-through. It is the story that explains the resume.

### "Why this company?" and "Why are you leaving?"

- **Why here:** specific to the team and its problems, not the brand. Read the job description, the engineering blog, and anything public about the team's systems, and connect them to your experience and your direction.
- **Why leaving:** forward-looking and honest. "I've learned a lot owning these services; I'm looking for a larger scope in distributed systems than my current team has" works. Criticising your current employer does not, even when justified.

### The project deep dive

Many hiring managers ask you to walk through one project in depth. Use your anchor story:

- **the problem and why it mattered**, in business terms;
- **the architecture**, drawn if possible, with your part marked;
- **the key decisions**, each with the alternatives you rejected;
- **what went wrong**, and what you did about it;
- **the result and what you would change.**

Expect technical probes. This is where a story you truly lived is indispensable.

### Your questions

Your questions are scored, and they are also your only chance to learn what the job is really like. Good questions are specific and show judgement:

- "What does a successful first six months look like for this role?"
- "What is the biggest technical risk the team is carrying right now?"
- "How does the team handle on-call, and what was the last significant incident?"
- "How are technical decisions made when engineers disagree?"
- "What does growth to the next level look like here, and who recently made it?"

Avoid questions answered on the careers page, and save compensation for the recruiter unless the manager raises it.

### Red flags and recovery

Behaviours that can outweigh good evidence:

- **Blaming others** for failures, in any story.
- **Disparaging a current or former employer.**
- **Inconsistency** between stories, or between a story and the resume.
- **Claiming credit for team work** that probes reveal was not yours.
- **No questions** at the end, which reads as low interest.

Recovery is possible within the round. If you realise you over-claimed, correct it: "I should be clearer - the design was mine, but the implementation was shared with two teammates." Correcting yourself is evidence of integrity, which the interviewer is also scoring.

### After the loop

Send a short thank-you note to the recruiter if that is the norm at the company. Write down, the same day, every question you were asked and how you answered; if you interview elsewhere, that is your best preparation material. If the outcome is negative, ask the recruiter for any feedback they can share.

## Failure modes and common mistakes

- **A resume walk-through** instead of a narrative.
- **Generic motivation**: "it's a great company."
- **Negative reasons for leaving.**
- **A deep dive without trade-offs** or without your part marked.
- **Weak or no questions.**

## Interview questions and model answers

**Q: Tell me about yourself.**
A: Now, how you got here, why this role - two minutes, one concrete achievement, and a direction that this role fits.

**Q: Why do you want to leave your current job?**
A: A forward-looking reason about the scope or kind of work you want next, stated without criticising your employer.

**Q: Walk me through a project you're proud of.**
A: The anchor story as a deep dive: problem, architecture with your part marked, decisions with alternatives, what went wrong, result, and what you would change.

**Q: Do you have any questions for me?**
A: Two or three specific questions - about the first six months, the team's biggest technical risk, and how disagreements are resolved.

## Exercises

1. Write and time your two-minute "tell me about yourself".
2. Write "why this company" for one real company you are targeting, naming the team's specific problems.
3. Draw the architecture of your anchor project and mark your part.
4. Write five questions to ask, and cross out any whose answer is on the company's website.

## Chapter summary

The hiring-manager round scores trajectory, motivation, and working fit alongside the behavioral signals. Prepare a two-minute narrative, specific reasons for this role, positive reasons for leaving, a project deep dive with your part and your decisions marked, and questions that show judgement. Avoid blame and inconsistency, and correct yourself openly if you over-claim.

## Revision checklist

- [ ] My "tell me about yourself" is two minutes and points forward.
- [ ] My "why here" names the team's specific problems.
- [ ] My anchor project is ready as a deep dive with decisions and alternatives.
- [ ] I have five questions that are not answered on the careers page.
